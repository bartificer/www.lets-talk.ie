<h5>Show Outline</h5>

<ul>
<li>In science an aperture is simply a hole in a surface.</li>
<li>In photography, the aperture is the width of the opening in the lens that the light passes through on its way to the focal plane where the image is recorded (on a sensor or on a piece of film)</li>
<li>Aperture is one of the three legs of the exposure triangle (along with exposure time and sensitivity, or ISO)</li>
<li>On many camera systems the size of the lens aperture can be controlled, but not on all

<ul>
<li>Smaller, simpler camera systems like those in phone cameras often have fixed apertures</li>
<li>Larger systems like Micro 4/3 and (D)SLR almost universally allow control of the lens aperture</li>
<li>Variable aperture is achieved with overlapping blades, and the number &amp; shape of these blades has noticeable effects:

<ul>
<li>Determines a lens&#8217;s bokeh</li>
<li>Determines the arrangement of a lens&#8217;s diffraction spikes (sunbursts etc.)</li>
</ul></li>
</ul></li>
<li>Aperture size is one of the factors that determines depth of field

<ul>
<li>When light passes through an aperture it get deflected a little (Fresnel diffraction). The amount of deflection depends on the size of the aperture:

<ul>
<li>A pinhole has effectively zero diffraction</li>
<li>The bigger the aperture, the more light gets diffracted</li>
<li>The more diffraction, the quicker focus falls off around the item that&#8217;s in focus</li>
<li>The bigger the physical hole, the shallower the depth of field</li>
</ul></li>
</ul></li>
<li>In photography, aperture size is represented as a <em>focal ratio</em> or <em>f-number</em>, not as a radius, diameter, or circumference as you might expect

<ul>
<li>Why?

<ul>
<li>Physical size of aperture is meaningless across camera systems

<ul>
<li>A 3mm aperture is tiny on a full-frame DSLR but massive on a camera phone!</li>
<li>Even on the same camera, a 3mm aperture on a wide-angle lens will result in a much brighter image than a 3mm aperture on a telephoto lens</li>
</ul></li>
<li>Focal ratio is more meaningful across more camera systems</li>
</ul></li>
<li>The focal ratio is also known as the <em>f-ratio</em>, <em>f-number</em> and <em>f-stop</em></li>
<li>The focal ratio is usually written as <em>f/N</em>, e.g. <em>f/1.4</em></li>
<li>The focal ratio is defined as the focal length of the lens divided by the diameter of the aperture</li>
<li>Because the focal ratio is a ratio it&#8217;s a dimensionless number, so you can&#8217;t mess up the units &#x1f642;</li>
<li>Notice that the bigger the aperture, the smaller the focal ratio!</li>
</ul></li>
<li>Why would you want to control aperture from an artistic point of view?

<ul>
<li>To control DOF

<ul>
<li>Sometimes you want to isolate your subject by having a shallow DOF, e.g. a portrait</li>
<li>Sometimes you want a really deep DOF to get a lot of things into focus, e.g. a landscape</li>
</ul></li>
<li>To let in as much light a possible in low-light extremes, e.g. astro photography</li>
<li>To let in as little light as possible to make longer exposures possible, e.g. trying to blur a waterfall or clouds moving across the sky</li>
</ul></li>
</ul>
