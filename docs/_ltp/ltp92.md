<h4>Show Notes</h4>

<p>It was great getting to talk with Antonio about the great railway photographer O. Winston Link on <a href="https://lets-talk.ie/ltp91">the previous show</a>. In that show we touched on the difference between photographing trains and photographing railways, but in the weeks since I felt I had more to say on the topic, so here we go &#x1f642;</p>

<h5>Part 1 — Railways -v- Trains</h5>

<ul>
<li>Railways are huge pieces of human and physical infrastructure.</li>
<li>Railways are deeply imbedded in their places.

<ul>
<li>Trains can&#8217;t handle steep grades or sharp turns, so the landscape has to be adapted to them!</li>
<li>Even railways that have been shut down for decades still leave their mark on the landscape.</li>
<li>The trains on a railway are heavily influenced by the railway&#8217;s place, and local needs. Passengers? Freight? Both? Fast? Slow? Long? Short? Diesel? Electric? Both? Short journeys? Long journeys?</li>
</ul></li>
<li>Railways are deeply imbedded in people&#8217;s lives.

<ul>
<li>Even today it takes quite a few humans to make a railway go!</li>
<li>People&#8217;s lives are regulated by railway timetables.</li>
</ul></li>
<li>I am interested in trains, but in the same way a car nut is interested in engines — they&#8217;re an important component, but they&#8217;re just one piece of a much bigger jigsaw!</li>
<li>I really like capturing the physical aspect of how railways interact with landscapes — trains in their places.</li>
<li>I also really like capturing a railways&#8217;s yearly cycles as the seasons come, go, and come again.</li>
<li>Some bigger railway components to think about capturing:

<ul>
<li>stations</li>
<li>signals and signal boxes</li>
<li>crossings</li>
<li>the tracks themselves</li>
<li>bridges &amp; tunnels</li>
</ul></li>
</ul>

<h5>Part 2 — Tips for Capturing Railway Shots with Trains</h5>

<ul>
<li><strong>DON&#8217;T TRESPASS ON THE LINE</strong> — no composition is worth your life!

<ul>
<li>Also, every train photographer who acts the goat puts more pressure on railway companies to put up ever uglier walls and fences, blocking off ever more possible views and compositions.</li>
<li>Depending on where you live, and how twitchy the railways company is, you might find yourself encountering law enforcement too. If you&#8217;re the wrong race in the wrong place with the wrong police force that could be just as fatal as getting hit by a train!</li>
</ul></li>
<li><strong>RESPECT PROPERTY, CROPS, LIVESTOCK, AND NATURE ALONG THE LINE SIDE</strong></li>
<li>Don&#8217;t rely on luck — take the time to really get to know a railway.

<ul>
<li>How closely do they run to time?</li>
<li>Timetables are per station, good shots are usually between stations, so you need to know how far your locations are from the stations either side</li>
<li>Do they have a real-time app?</li>
<li>Do they run different rolling stock at different times of the day or week?</li>
<li>How well can you hear trains coming at your locations?

<ul>
<li>Are there whistle boards nearby, and if so, how long will it be between hearing the horn and the train arriving?</li>
<li>Is there a level crossing nearby who&#8217;s bells/sirens you can hear? If so, how much warning does that give you?</li>
</ul></li>
</ul></li>
<li>Plan, plan, and plan some more! Trains literally run to a timetable — you have to be ready when they make their appearance, because you have zero control!

<ul>
<li>Take note of how the direction of light changes throughout the day, and as the seasons change – the railway doesn&#8217;t move, but the sun does, so different shots become possible at different times!</li>
<li>Bear in mind that cloudy days open up angles and compositions that would normally be <em>against</em> the sun</li>
</ul></li>
<li>It&#8217;s all about finding good foregrounds, backgrounds, and framing features — a railway shot with a train is about showing the train in a place, so the stuff around the train is at least as important as the train itself, if not more!</li>
<li>Take test shots while you wait for the train

<ul>
<li>If conditions and gear allow, use manual exposure control

<ul>
<li>Train headlights can really bias light meters, leading to underexposed shots</li>
<li>Not possible in variable cloud where the sun could come and go any instant!</li>
</ul></li>
</ul></li>
<li>Be careful not to let your exposure times drop — trains move, unless you want them blurred, you need to have short exposures!</li>
</ul>
