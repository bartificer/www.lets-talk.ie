<p>In <a href="https://www.lets-talk.ie/blog/?p=73022">the previous episode</a> I put the so-called <em>Rule of Thirds</em> into what I believe is the correct context - it&#8217;s not a rule, it&#8217;s a guideline, and it&#8217;s just one possible way of achieving a nice composition in some situations — it&#8217;s not the universal key to good photographs. Not all photographs that obey the rule are good, and nowhere near all good photographs obey the rule!</p>

<p>There are lots more equally good guidelines out there that are just as helpful as the Rule of Thirds, but they don&#8217;t have catchy names. So, in this episode I&#8217;m going to try share some of my favourites, and I&#8217;m going to try give them catchy names to help you remember them &#x1f642;</p>

<h4>What Makes a Good Composition?</h4>

<p>Before digging into some guidelines to help improve your compositions, we should probably define what it even means for a composition to be good!</p>

<p>In my mind a good composition achieves three things:</p>

<ol>
<li>It makes the photograph catch people&#8217;s attention — with so many images all around us we can&#8217;t pay attention to them all, so, we&#8217;re only going to see the ones that somehow catch our eye.</li>
<li>Guides the user&#8217;s eye to where the photographer wants them to look, either — that can take all sorts of forms, maybe the photographer wants your attention drawn to a single thing, maybe they want to draw you along a specific path through the image, or maybe they just want to pull you into the world of the image and let your eye wander and explore.</li>
<li>Gives a sense of depth — a photograph is a 2D representation of a 3D world. A good composition captures and communicates the sense of the missing third dimension to the viewer.</li>
</ol>

<p>There are lots of guidelines we can use to help us achieve these goals. A good image often combines many of them.</p>

<p>The rule of thirds may be the flashiest, but don&#8217;t let it&#8217;s bling distract you! </p>

<h4>Breaking the Rule of Thirds</h4>

<p>Sometimes, the best thing you can do is intentionally break the rule of thirds. My mental photography toolbox contains three guidelines that directly contradict it, and one that usually does.</p>

<h5>Centre Reflections</h5>

<p>The ultimate essence of a reflection is symmetry — if you&#8217;re trying to capture that it really helps to centre the horizon in your frame and keep it dead level (that goes double when there&#8217;s water involved). </p>

<p>If your subject is circular, try photograph it face-on and then centre it right in your composition. </p>

<p>This work particularly well within a square aspect ratio.</p>

<h5>Give Room (for thing to Look &amp; Move Into)</h5>

<p>Something with an obvious front, or something obviously moving will feel uncomfortable unless it has some room to look or move into within the composition. This doesn&#8217;t just apply to people and animals, but also to flowers, trains, cars, planes …</p>

<p>The more something is &#8216;looking&#8217; or moving to the side, the more room it needs to feel in balance, so the rule of thirds is very counter-productive here.</p>

<h5>Dig those Diagonals</h5>

<p>The rule of thirds is all about aligning things along a horizontal grid. Why limit yourself to that grid? Why not align your composition along lines running from corner to corner?</p>

<p>Compositions with strong diagonals can be very powerful. In theory you could use a strong diagonal in your composition and still put the main subject at one of the crossing points on the rule of thirds grid, but you really shouldn&#8217;t feel any sort of pressure to do so. Other considerations like providing a comfortable-feeling amount of room to look into are usually much more important!</p>

<p>Diagonals can be obvious linear features in your images:</p>

<h4>Guidelines for Guiding the Eye</h4>

<p>This next group of guidelines are useful in compositions where you want to draw the viewer&#8217;s attention to specific things, or to guide their attention along a particular route.</p>

<p>You can use strong obvious lines to direct the viewer&#8217;s eye where you want it within a photograph.</p>

<p>Having a path start at the bottom of the frame and then recede off into the distance can really help pull a viewer&#8217;s eye into a photo. Paths pulling the viewer&#8217;s eyes off into the distance are also a great way of adding a sense of depth to a photo.</p>

<h4>Guides for Interesting Landscapes</h4>

<p>When you&#8217;re trying to capture a sense of place you very often don&#8217;t want to guide the viewer&#8217;s eye to a specific thing, but instead, to entice them in and then let them roam around and explore on their own. To achieve that you really need a sense of depth.</p>

<p>Also, to capture a good feel of a place it&#8217;s often very useful to capture features at multiple scales — a flower, a field, and a field-pattern perhaps.</p>

<p>I find interesting landscapes particularly challenging, so I force myself to think in terms of my three-layers rule.</p>

<h5>Bart&#8217;s 3 Layers Rule</h5>

<p>When shooting landscapes I&#8217;m always trying to meet three criteria when ever I can:</p>

<ol>
<li>An interesting foreground feature — an artefact, a plant, an animal, a geological feature, or even an interesting texture or shadow.</li>
<li>A a mid-ground that gives a good sense of the place — rolling hills, a wide expansive plane, a field …</li>
<li>A nice backdrop — a distant part of the landscape, an interesting horizon or an impressive sky.</li>
</ol>

<p>The mid-ground is usually the easy part, it&#8217;s the foreground that often proves the most problematic in my experience – that&#8217;s where the remaining guidelines in this episode come in.</p>

<h5>Find a Frame</h5>

<p>Finding something to frame a corner of an image, a side of an image, or even all four sides of an image can really help to give a shot a sense of depth. This works especially well for landscapes where you&#8217;ll usually find an obliging tree.</p>

<h5>Go Low!</h5>

<p>In the real world when they go low I try to go high, but photography is different &#x1f609;</p>

<p>We usually see the world from eye-height. Getting down low to the ground literally gives us a new perspective, remember that!</p>
